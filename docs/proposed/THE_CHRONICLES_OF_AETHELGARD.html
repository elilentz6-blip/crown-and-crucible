Prologue — The Levy for the Empty Tower

The widow came to the record house with a piece of lead in her basket.

It was the eighteenth autumn of King Aldous III’s reign, in the year four hundred and eighty-seven by the Crown reckoning. Rain had entered through the eastern shutters, and we had spread the household accounts of three dead kings along the floor to dry. A clerk was carrying the oldest volumes upstairs. He stepped over the woman’s basket twice before asking whether she had come to sell something.

She said she had come about the tower.

There were several towers within sight of the record house. He asked which.

“The one we pay for.”

Her name was Maud Hessel. She lived in the Greenmere Vale, two days from High Cairn in good weather, though the crossing had taken her four. Her husband had died five winters earlier. Her eldest son maintained their holding, and her younger son was serving under a captain whose present whereabouts she did not know. The piece of lead had been cut from the roof of the old western watchtower above Stonecross. Beneath the dirt, one could still distinguish the little impression of a crowned hand.

For three generations, her household had contributed to the provision of that tower. She had brought the receipt for the latest payment, folded inside a scrap of linen.

There had been no garrison there for twenty-six years.

At first I supposed she wanted the levy remitted. The clerk supposed the same and explained that remissions were granted by the appropriate officers, whose names he offered to write down.

“I want to know who was supposed to stay,” she said.

We found the foundation entry that afternoon. The tower had been raised in the one hundred and ninety-second year of the Crown, during the reign of Queen Aveline, when ships could still come farther up the river and an armed company on the western road might reach the grain land before warning arrived from the coast. Six households furnished labor. Two furnished carts. The quarry belonged to a religious house which surrendered the stone in return for confirmation of its meadow rights. A neighboring lord supplied the timber and afterward claimed that this contribution made the tower his.

The queen’s officers disagreed.

A settlement was reached. The tower would remain in royal keeping; the lord might name its captain, subject to the sovereign’s assent; the surrounding households would provide certain victuals and repairs; and the religious house would retain its meadows, except for a strip beside the water where the garrison’s animals could drink.

The arrangement occupied less than a page. Its consequences filled four cupboards.

There were petitions concerning the drinking place, complaints about horses entering the meadow, a dispute over the captain’s choice of timber, and a judgment against a cooper who had supplied leaking barrels. One woman had asked to substitute two days’ labor for a measure of oats because her crop had failed. Another had asked that the same substitution be forbidden because her husband would be taken from their own harvest.

The last captain’s accounts were more difficult to find.

He had left after the Truce of Ryehaven, when the field companies were withdrawn and each lord declared that the duty of watching the roads had returned to someone else. He had petitioned for wages, then for food, then for permission to reduce his company. In the final surviving letter, he reported that his men were cutting the roof lead into saleable pieces.

“I have forbidden them,” he wrote, “but I cannot promise to forbid them tomorrow.”

By that winter, the tower was empty.

Maud listened while I read. When I had finished, she asked whether the captain had been paid.

I could find no acquittance.

She put the receipt back inside the linen. She left the lead on my desk.

I have it still.

That winter I went to the tower. An elder bush had grown through the stair. Rainwater stood in the chamber where the watch had slept, and someone had made a hearth against the old arrow slit. The upper floor was gone. From the broken wall I could see the river turning toward Stonecross, a patch of winter-sown land, and a cart moving so slowly along the road that it seemed at first to be standing still.

A boy gathering fallen wood told me that the tower was haunted. He had heard knocking there after dark. His grandfather said it was the watch asking to be relieved.

The knocking came again while we spoke. A loose timber was shifting in the wind.

The boy watched it for a while.

“My grandfather heard it before that beam was loose,” he said.

I did not argue with him. His grandfather had worked on the repairs, and his grandfather’s father had done the same. Their account of the tower deserved a place beside the captain’s letters.

I had then been employed among records for thirty-one years. I knew the hands of chancery clerks who had been dead longer than my father had lived. I could distinguish a copied seal from a genuine one, and sometimes a genuine seal from an honest document. Yet until Maud Hessel set her basket on the floor, I had never followed one obligation from the person who paid it to the authority in whose name it was collected.

The levy was still lawful, according to the officers who received it. Part of its revenue had been pledged against money borrowed for repairs elsewhere. The captain’s departure had not extinguished the pledge. The households had not been parties to that later bargain, but their payments supported it.

To explain this to the widow required a knowledge of two reigns, a disputed inheritance, a war that the books called finished, and a debt whose original lender had died before she married.

It seemed to me that a history of the kingdom might begin there.

My name is Oren of Halewick. My father caulked boats and expected me to learn the same trade. A teacher at the House of the Witness discovered that I could remember words more readily than I could shape timber, and the house sent me to serve a man who needed a clerk. From him I passed into royal employment. The Crown has fed me for much of my life. A reader may reasonably remember this when I speak of its enemies.

I have taken what I can from its archives, from the records of religious houses, from toll books, household rolls, merchants’ reckonings, and the testimony of people who have never seen a royal seal. Where these disagree, I have tried to preserve the disagreement. A man who writes that a village was deserted may mean that its inhabitants fled; he may mean that they were driven out; he may mean that they ceased paying him.

For dates before the first Crown settlement, I have used the reckonings accepted by the keepers of the oldest books, though they do not always agree with one another. I have translated names where translation makes the account intelligible. Some passages attributed to the earliest speakers were first written down centuries after their deaths. I will say so when I use them.

King Aldous is our lawful sovereign. There are men presently gathering soldiers who acknowledge this as readily as I do. Their quarrels concern what obedience requires, what protection has been promised, which customs bind the Crown, and which promises were made by persons entitled to make them. Some would profit by breaking the kingdom. Others may break it while attempting to preserve it.

The fighting has continued, in one place or another, for almost a hundred years. In sheltered districts there are men of middle age who have never seen a field army. Across the water from them are children who can tell which company is passing by the sound of its carts.

I shall come to those years in their proper place.

First it is necessary to consider the people who crossed these waters before a king could claim them, and the people who were already waiting on the shore.

Book I — Before the Crown

Chapter One — The Shore Where They Stayed

Along the southern shore of Sere, the dead are occasionally uncovered by the sea.

A winter storm cuts into a low bank, the turf slips, and a skull appears among the roots. Sometimes there is a fragment of worked bone or a green stain where a small fastening has perished. The fishermen rebury what they find. In the older villages they do so without ringing a bell, because, they say, no one can know what prayers those people understood.

This has not prevented learned men from supplying them with ancestors.

The royal genealogy drawn up under King Edric claimed that the first islanders descended from his own forebears. A later writer in the service of a western lord argued that they were the forebears of everyone except the kings. Both accounts named the same ancient graves as proof. Neither author had visited Sere.

We have less knowledge of the earliest inhabitants than either possessed in his own estimation. Their walls survive beneath later walls. Their burial places lie under fields whose names belong to a different tongue. On the western headlands stand low enclosures so heavy with fallen stone that a stranger might take them for the work of giants. A woman who has carried water up to one of them is more likely to wonder how many hands were needed and how far they had to go for a drink.

They kept cattle of a small, hardy kind, grew grain where the soil allowed, and took much of their living from the shore. Their descendants left names for channels, springs, and dangerous rocks which later arrivals continued to use. These names outlasted the names of rulers. A fisherman has reason to preserve the word for a reef even after the household that first warned him of it has disappeared.

The people whom the earliest surviving books call the Leven were among those older inhabitants, though the word should not persuade us that they were one people under one authority. It appears to have been a name used by outsiders for several coastal communities. Their own names are mostly lost.

What the later records preserve suggests settlements joined by kinship, marriages, seasonal gatherings, and quarrels over land and water. A headman’s influence might reach from one bay to the next and fail at the intervening ridge. A man powerful enough to take cattle could still need his neighbors’ consent to bring them home through their grazing ground.

The islands were inhabited. They were also continually receiving people.

Some arrivals came with enough followers to impose terms. Others came looking for someone willing to employ them. There were marriages across channels, fishing crews blown out of their accustomed waters, captives brought home from raids, and families whose reasons for departure were remembered differently by those who left and those who remained.

Among the accounts of these movements, none has been copied more often than the story of the woman at the oar.

The oldest written version belongs to the records of the House at Merrow on Sere. It was copied in the seventy-third year of the Crown from a book which the copyist said was already damaged. The compiler placed the arrival in the third century before the Crown. Later genealogists supplied an exact year, then disagreed about it.

In this account, twenty-two people reached the southern shore after three nights at sea. Their boat had come from the coast called Istr, beyond the western passage. They carried two goats, a chest of household iron, a small quantity of grain, and the body of a boy.

The woman was named Nera.

The Merrow book calls her “Nera who held the steering oar.” It does not call her a queen, a chief’s daughter, or a woman of remarkable beauty. Those particulars were added when important families discovered that she might be an agreeable ancestor. One pedigree gives her three sons. Another gives her seven. The boy who died at sea is absent from both.

Of the voyage itself we know little. The boat was an open vessel with a single mast. It had been caulked badly or in haste, for the account says that the passengers were bailing throughout the second night. An old woman wrapped the seed grain inside her clothing and sat upon it to keep the water out. The sail tore before they sighted land.

Why they had departed is disputed.

The Merrow compiler says that armed men had taken the previous harvest along the Istrian coast and that the people of three settlements had been required to provide food for another winter. A song preserved much later says that Nera fled a marriage. A family history claims that her husband had been killed for refusing service to a lord.

These accounts need not describe the same person. There were many departures.

The taking of grain nevertheless offers a credible explanation for the condition in which the boat arrived. Its people were hungry, poorly supplied, and unwilling to turn back. Whatever they had expected to find across the passage, they had brought seed with them.

They came ashore beneath a chalk-colored headland at a place now called Bracken Mouth. The stream there divides around a shingle bar. At low water it is easy to cross; at high water the channel becomes deep enough to admit a shallow boat. Behind the beach lay a narrow stretch of workable ground, sheltered by rising land and already occupied.

Smoke was visible beyond the ridge.

The newcomers pulled their boat above the wet shingle. Someone carried the dead boy to a patch of dry ground. The goats were tethered to the broken mast.

By the following morning, men from the neighboring settlement were watching them.

Later painters have represented this meeting with a lord on horseback extending a welcoming hand. There is no horse in the Merrow account. The local men carried spears, and the strangers’ iron chest remained closed.

A man called Edran appears to have spoken for the people who held the ground. His name is given in the form used by the compiler, who may have translated it. He asked where the strangers intended to go.

The reply attributed to Nera is: “We intended to arrive.”

It is a good answer, which may be why someone eventually gave it to her.

They had no common speech sufficient for bargaining. One of the newcomers had traded dried fish along part of the Istrian coast and knew words resembling a few used on Sere. He interpreted with gestures, repeated names, and quantities laid out upon the ground.

The local men pointed to the fields.

The strangers pointed to the boat.

When the tide rose, water entered through the damaged seams.

The council which followed has been enlarged by nearly every teller. In the earliest account, it took place at the settlement above the ridge and concerned shelter for a single winter. No surviving witness could have recorded it. The compiler was setting down what later households remembered of the terms under which their predecessors had stayed.

Those terms were neither an act of conquest nor a gift of empty land.

The strangers could occupy the old cattle enclosure beside the stream. They could repair its wall and roof a shelter within it. They might fish from the beach, but the places where nets were set between stakes belonged to particular households. They could gather fallen wood. Standing timber required permission. The goats were to be kept away from the winter grazing.

In return, they would help with the fishing, repair the bank where the stream flooded the lower field, and surrender part of what they caught.

Nothing was said about the following year.

The absence mattered.

Nera’s company spent the first weeks lifting wet stones from the enclosure and cutting material for a roof. They used the sail where the thatch would not reach. The weather worsened. One goat sickened and was killed. The old woman who had kept the seed dry died before the shortest day.

The compiler gives her name as Vedda.

She had carried the grain across the sea, but she did not live to see it planted.

For food, the newcomers depended upon their own fishing and the willingness of their hosts to advance grain against labor. Such advances were remembered as generosity in the descendants of those who received them and as debts in the descendants of those who supplied them. Long afterward, when the settlement had become a manor, both accounts were produced before a court.

That court found the obligations established by long observance. It did not attempt to discover what either party had understood during the first winter.

There was a dispute over the dead boy.

His people wanted to bury him near the enclosure. Some of the local households objected. Their own dead lay farther inland, above the spring, and they feared that an unfamiliar burial might offend those already there. Others thought the objection foolish. The strangers had brought a corpse ashore; they could scarcely keep it with them until everyone agreed.

Here the Merrow writer’s own beliefs enter the story. He says that Nera received instruction in the remembrance of the dead and accepted the local burial place. A later account describes her conversion to the true Observance and records a prayer for which there is no early authority.

What can reasonably be retained is that the boy was buried above the spring.

No object found there identifies his grave. The people of Bracken Mouth pointed one out to me, a low stone beneath a thorn tree. They have been pointing out that stone for longer than anyone remembers. The tree is younger than the church.

The story of the burial was useful because it explained why the strangers had a place among the local dead before they had secure possession of a field.

By the end of winter, possession of a field had become the more difficult matter.

The newcomers needed ground on which to sow the grain they had brought. Edran could permit them to use the cattle enclosure, but he could not dispose of every piece of land around it. A wet strip beyond the stream was cultivated in favorable years by his sister’s household. Higher ground was grazed by several families. The patch nearest the shore was poor, stony, and subject to salt water.

This last they were allowed to break.

There were objections even then. A household which had contributed little to feeding the newcomers claimed that its children gathered shellfish beside the same ground and would be hindered by a fence. Another demanded compensation for the use of a path. Edran’s sister argued that if the strangers improved their strip they would presently ask to extend it into hers.

In the songs, a generous ruler bestows the land with a sweep of his arm.

In the record, the boundary follows the path.

It follows it awkwardly, narrowing where the shellfish gatherers wished to pass and turning around a damp hollow that no one wanted. When, centuries later, a steward tried to straighten it, three households opposed him. By then they had forgotten the strangers and remembered only the shape of their rights.

The seed was sown late.

We are told that Nera carried the first measure herself. This may have been a family remembrance. It may have been added by a writer who understood what readers would wish her to do. A woman who had helped bail an open boat for two nights would certainly have been capable of carrying a basket across a field.

The harvest was poor.

This detail has survived the efforts of several later writers to make the whole account more splendid. The strangers had cleared too little ground, lost time over its boundaries, and planted grain brought from a different shore. Some seed failed. Wind damaged the young growth. What they gathered could not support them through another winter without fishing and further advances from their neighbors.

Their presence had nevertheless begun to alter the settlement.

One man from the boat knew how to repair a kind of iron fastening used in hulls. Another made pots with a narrow shoulder unlike those commonly used on Sere. A young woman entered the household of a local fisherman. Whether she went as wife, servant, or dependent is not stated.

People learned enough of each other’s speech to quarrel without an interpreter.

The newcomers’ shelter was rebuilt. A second hearth appeared. The goats, reduced to one after the first winter, were joined by a young animal whose ownership became the subject of a complaint. A boy born after the arrival was given a local name.

No council assembled to declare that temporary guests had become inhabitants.

The following year came, and they were there.

Those who consider the settlement the beginning of the Istrian conquest of Sere must account for the local names its descendants continued to use, the marriages they made, and the permissions they still sought. Those who say that the strangers were absorbed without consequence must account for the speech which spread along that shore and the families which later claimed that their obligations were different because their forebears had arrived by sea.

Their children inherited a mixture of advantages and burdens.

They had a place in the settlement and a claim to the cultivated strip. They also inherited duties whose origin could be told in several ways. A gift of food might become a debt; assistance freely offered during a hard winter might become customary service. The descendants of the hosts could forget their own need for skilled hands. The descendants of the guests could forget who had fed them.

When disputes arose, each household found an ancestor who had understood the bargain exactly as it now wished to understand it.

We should be cautious about deciding which was right.

The Bracken Mouth agreement concerned a few hungry people and a poor strip of soil. Similar agreements were made elsewhere under other pressures. Sometimes newcomers were numerous enough to seize what they wanted. Sometimes local households used them against a neighboring settlement. A man who would refuse strangers a pasture might offer it readily if they agreed to carry spears in his quarrel.

On the larger island of Caerwyn, later traditions describe the arrival of armed households whose leaders married into families already holding the river crossings. On Terveth, fishing communities preserved claims that their ancestors had admitted outsiders only to work the boats. These stories were still being used when royal officers first came to reckon obligations.

The officers found no single custom of settlement.

They found people living beside people whose grandparents had come from somewhere else. They found fields held by consent, force, marriage, purchase, or an arrangement which had begun as one thing and become another. A headman might be powerful in the summer gathering and unable to compel a particular household when its neighbors supported its refusal.

The later kings inherited these difficulties. Their clerks gave many of them more regular names.

This is why I have lingered over a settlement whose people possessed no crown, founded no dynasty that can be proved, and left no account in their own hands. So much of what followed depended upon bargains resembling theirs. The terms could survive long after the circumstances that had made them tolerable were gone.

Nera herself disappears from the early account.

One later song says that she drowned while bringing cattle home from another island. A pedigree says that she lived to see a grandson rule the southern shore. The Merrow book records neither her death nor a second voyage. Its last notice concerns the repair of the enclosure wall.

There is a house at Bracken Mouth whose outer boundary still follows part of that enclosure. The family living there has no wish to claim descent from her. They say their grandfather purchased the holding from a man who had debts.

When I visited, the woman of the house was washing grit from greens in a wooden bowl. She knew the story of the boat, though she called its steerswoman Nerren and gave the dead boy a name I had not heard elsewhere. Her husband was repairing a fish trap. The tide was beginning to turn.

She showed me where the old path passed between the holding and the shore.

I asked whether people still used it.

“When they want the beach,” she said.

The bowl was heavy. I held it while she opened the gate.

Chapter Two — The Water Between

At its narrowest, the passage between Caerwyn and Maroven can be crossed before a man has finished telling an unwelcome story. In winter, a boat may wait six days to attempt it.

This inconvenience has defeated many theories of the kingdom. A lord looking at a chart sees his estates separated by a strip of blue. His reeve, charged with delivering twenty sacks of grain across that strip, must find a boat, an available master, a place to load, a favorable tide, and someone prepared to answer for the cargo if the voyage goes badly. The lord’s authority is present at each stage. It cannot supply the wind.

Before the Crown, these waters carried forms of power that were difficult to draw upon parchment.

The older rulers of Caerwyn held the mouths of rivers and the defensible heights above them. On Maroven, a family with grain land might hire boats from men whom it could not otherwise command. Among the western islands, a man’s following depended partly upon the households willing to beach their vessels beside his. A claim to a channel was of little use if the pilots refused to bring a ship through it.

The first rulers to extend their influence beyond a single island often did so by becoming useful to people who had no wish to be their subjects. They protected a winter anchorage, punished a cattle raid, or took hostages to secure a disputed marriage payment. Their followers expected a share of what these interventions produced. A successful leader needed further occasions on which to reward them.

The distinction between protector and raider consequently depended upon the shore from which a person watched him approach.

A story told on Terveth concerns a leader named Ossan who demanded food from a settlement he had recently defended. The inhabitants replied that his enemies had taken the food during the attack.

“Then you must defend me against hunger,” he is said to have answered.

The tale has been attached to three different men. The problem it describes was common enough.

In the generation before Maeric, two powers had begun to dominate the inner passages. The lords of Talven held fertile ground beside the Lower Elde on Caerwyn, together with a landing sheltered from the prevailing winds. Their neighbors at Greyhaven controlled the outer entrance. Boats traveling upriver could reach Talven more safely if Greyhaven’s men left them alone.

This gave both households a reason to claim the same revenues.

Their quarrel drew in carriers from Terveth and landholders on Ordel who needed a market for grain. It also drew in the religious house at Harth, whose brothers maintained a guest hall beside the crossing. The house depended upon gifts from families on both sides. Its lands suffered when either side marched.

The people at Harth began keeping copies of agreements because the parties had acquired the habit of forgetting them.

Several such copies survive. They concern compensation for drowned cattle, payment for a damaged vessel, and the return of a girl held as security for her father’s undertaking. There is no grand declaration of island unity. One settlement provides that a boat carrying seed grain may pass Greyhaven without seizure, even when the men on board owe service to Talven.

The rule made practical sense. A boat might contain goods belonging to half a dozen households, including people whom Greyhaven could not afford to offend.

No one had yet solved the larger difficulty. Each peace depended upon the persons who had sworn it, the hostages available to enforce it, and the next season’s balance of strength.

When a son inherited, he inherited both his father’s enemies and an opportunity to deny his father’s bargains.

It was under these circumstances that Maeric, son of Gaerwid of Talven, first appears in a surviving account. He is named among the young men required to remain at Greyhaven while his father’s boats passed through the channel.

He was sixteen.

The account records the food supplied to the hostages. Maeric received the same allowance as the others. Nothing in it suggests that the keeper of the household recognized a future king.

Book II — The Forging of Aethelgard

Chapter Three — A King Who Needed Boats

Maeric’s father died seven years before the Crown reckoning begins. His inheritance consisted of land, a hall, claims to several crossings, and obligations which consumed much of their value.

The Greyhaven quarrel was unsettled. Three neighboring households had been promised compensation. An uncle held part of the family’s ground for life and considered his nephew insufficiently attentive to this arrangement. Maeric possessed men willing to fight for him, but their willingness diminished when their own planting required attention.

The earliest record of his rule is an order that two boats be repaired before anything was spent on enlarging the hall.

This choice was remembered. Later household writers made it an example of his foresight. It may equally have been an example of necessity: the timber had already been purchased, and the boatmen were threatening to leave.

He spent the following years arranging passages that his father had tried to command.

A bargain with the carriers of Terveth gave him vessels in return for winter shelter and compensation when he detained them beyond an agreed term. With the grain holders of Ordel he promised a hearing before seizure of cargo. At Harth, he accepted witnesses drawn from households outside his immediate following.

These concessions did not make him gentle. When a Greyhaven captain took a vessel protected by his undertaking, Maeric attacked the captain’s landing at low water and burned boats which could not be launched. The action cost him men and destroyed property he might otherwise have acquired. It also demonstrated that his assurances were expensive to disregard.

His marriage helped make them credible.

Aelis of Ordel brought interests her husband could neither absorb nor ignore. Her family, the Morns, held grain land and marriage ties among several competing households. Her widow’s provision, agreed before the wedding, was secured upon revenues that Maeric had intended to use for his own followers.

Her seal appears beside his on two agreements. A later copy omits it. The original of one remains at Harth, where the hole made by its attachment can still be seen.

It is customary to describe this marriage as the joining of grain and ships. Aelis’s surviving correspondence is less accommodating. She complained that the masters of the ships reckoned their days differently according to whether they were owed wages or required to sail.

In the second year before the Crown, Greyhaven closed the passage again. Its lord, Hadran, claimed compensation for Maeric’s attack. He had a defensible shore, but little grain land. The closure interrupted the provisions upon which his own following depended.

Maeric did not immediately attack. He brought boats around the southern headland and supplied rival landings. He paid pilots who knew the shallow channels. Aelis secured grain on terms that obliged him to continue the campaign until the following harvest.

Hadran’s household divided. Men whose livelihood depended upon the passage urged a settlement. Others demanded an assault on Talven before Maeric’s borrowed provisions were exhausted.

The assault came too late.

At the ford below Harth, Hadran’s force encountered a crossing defended by men from three households which had not previously served together. The fight lasted much of a morning. A mill race impeded one flank; a broken cart blocked part of the approach. Later songs supplied thousands of warriors. The victual account supports fewer than four hundred on Maeric’s side.

Hadran was taken alive. His eldest son was killed in the retreat.

The settlement made at Harth the following spring became the beginning of our reckoning.

Seven anchorages sent representatives. These were not seven entire islands. The first agreement bound a mixture of coastal lords, landholders, carriers, and the keepers of Harth. Some signed because they wanted protection. Some wanted compensation. Hadran’s surviving son signed because Maeric held his father.

Maeric was acknowledged as king over the peace they had undertaken to maintain.

The document required him to hear disputes between its participants, protect passages named in it, and refrain from taking their property outside specified customs. In return, they owed assistance, counsel, and defined contributions to the keeping of the peace.

A king’s power had been established by persons who took care to describe its limits.

They did not employ the language of our later courts. They could not have foreseen all that would be claimed under their seals. The agreement nevertheless contained the difficulty which every succeeding reign has inherited: the Crown needed resources sufficient to protect its adherents, while its adherents intended that protection to cost them as little authority as possible.

The crown used at Maeric’s acknowledgment was narrow and plain. The goldsmith’s account does not survive. The account for the feast does. It includes payment for the repair of a roof damaged by men climbing upon it to see the king.

Chapter Four — The Price of Submission

The kingdom was not completed at Harth.

Sere acknowledged Maeric in the fourteenth year, after two western settlements appealed to him against a neighboring lord. He arrived with enough force to make refusal dangerous and enough debt to make prolonged occupation impossible. The agreement left local inheritances and household obligations to local judgment, reserving certain disputes among the greater lords for his hearing.

On Maroven, submission followed marriage negotiations and a campaign against men who had seized the Greenmere river crossings. The nineteenth-year settlement preserved customary passage for grain boats, confirmed the rights of existing holders, and provided royal assistance against attacks from outside the island.

The victors called it restoration. The family displaced from the crossings called it theft. Both accounts were copied by religious houses which had received land from different sides.

Vey came into the settlement in the twenty-seventh year. Its landing places were small, its approaches difficult, and its people useful as pilots. Maeric’s officers proposed an assessment based upon the size of the island. The islanders proposed one based upon what they could actually furnish. Their undertaking was finally expressed in boats and service, with exceptions when weather made sailing impossible.

Those exceptions have occupied more lawyers than the original assessment.

Rusk resisted longer. Its lord, Sovan Arven, held hill defenses which a force dependent upon imported provisions could not easily reduce. Maeric could burn lower settlements, but each burning deprived him of food and made his return less welcome.

The campaign of the thirtieth year nearly ruined him. A storm scattered the transports. Men who had completed their agreed service demanded release. Aelis sent grain and a letter which a later admirer of the king wished had been lost.

“You may retain Rusk after you have taken it,” she wrote, “but you cannot eat its stones while you are doing so.”

Maeric accepted negotiations.

Sovan acknowledged the Crown in the thirty-first year. He retained his family’s lands, courts, and local following. The king received an undertaking of military assistance and the right to hear certain disputes involving other great holders. A royal garrison was admitted to a coastal fort, supplied under terms which specified whose grain would feed it.

The submission was neither independence nor surrender of every right. Rusk’s later lords would insist upon one half of this truth whenever a king insisted upon the other.

Maeric died three years afterward.

His son succeeded as Maeric II. The succession was supported by his mother, by the principal households at Harth, and by men whose interests were now tied to the first settlement. It still required journeys, confirmations, gifts, and assurances. A dead man’s crown did not carry itself across the sea.

Several holders demanded renewed guarantees before acknowledging the son.

Aelis advised him to give them.

“He must promise what he can keep,” she told the keeper at Harth, “and learn what they have promised him.”

She retained her own estates and revenues. Her son consulted her until she died. Later accounts praise his filial devotion without discussing how many of his boats she could otherwise have left unpaid.

Under Maeric II, the kingdom acquired more regular places of record. Agreements were copied together. Payments were compared. Men began to discover that an undertaking made by a grandfather could be produced against a grandson.

This was useful to the Crown. It was also useful against the Crown.

Aethelgard appears as the kingdom’s name in a surviving document of this reign, although the copy may have modernized an older word. Court writers translate it as the noble keeping. Western commentators prefer a meaning associated with guarded passages. The surviving evidence does not permit certainty.

The name traveled farther than the king’s officers.

Merchants used it when dealing abroad because a common designation could be convenient even when common protection was absent. Religious writers adopted it in prayers for peace. Lords used it when defending a right they considered older than the king who disputed it.

By the time of Maeric II’s death, there were people who could imagine the islands belonging to one kingdom without imagining that they all belonged to the king in the same way.

Book III — The Age of Kings

Chapter Five — The Inheritance of the Daughters

The third sovereign, Osric I, inherited a realm accustomed to consultation and spent much of his reign determining whom he was required to consult.

He traveled more than his father. At the greater landings he held hearings in halls borrowed from men whose conduct might be among the matters heard. His household carried bedding, writing materials, vessels for the table, and enough armed followers to make its presence burdensome.

A visit could confer honor and consume the provisions reserved for winter.

When Osric died in the ninety-third year, his daughter Edra was the only surviving child born of a marriage acknowledged by the principal religious authorities. His brothers’ descendants objected that custom favored a man capable of leading the host. Edra’s supporters answered that these same cousins had accepted land through women when it suited them.

The argument was not settled by discovering an ancient rule which everyone had overlooked.

Edra held her father’s household, the treasury available at his death, and the support of several men who feared her cousins more than they feared a woman’s rule. She confirmed their rights. The keepers at Harth acknowledged her. Ordel supplied provisions through her mother’s kin.

She was crowned before the opposition could agree upon a single claimant.

Her first campaign was commanded by a lord who owed much of his inheritance to the marriage settlement she was defending. She remained near the principal crossing, receiving petitions, arranging supplies, and requiring reports. The campaign was remembered afterward as his victory. The surviving letters show that he repeatedly asked her for money.

Edra’s reign established a precedent, though it did not produce agreement about its meaning. Her supporters said that the nearest lawful heir might be a woman. Her opponents’ descendants said that the great holders had chosen an exceptional remedy during a dangerous season.

Both interpretations remained available when Aveline inherited almost a century later.

Between the two queens, Maeric III and Edric I enlarged royal administration. More obligations were entered in books. Places of hearing became more regular. A man wronged by his immediate lord sometimes sought a royal officer, whose willingness to intervene depended upon the kind of wrong, the claimant’s standing, and the Crown’s interest.

These changes did not reach every island equally.

On Sere, local witnesses still carried greater weight than documents which they could not read. In parts of Ordel, great estates kept increasingly detailed accounts of customary labor. Some chartered settlements purchased exemptions in return for agreed payments. The Crown’s officers could recognize one freedom without making it universal.

Aveline became queen in the one hundred and eighty-eighth year. Her husband had died four years earlier. She brought to the succession a mature household and the experience of defending her own property against his relations.

She had no intention of repeating the argument that a woman could hold an estate but not the authority required to preserve it.

Chapter Six — Stone and Water

The western tower above Stonecross belongs to Aveline’s reign. So does the first royal confirmation of Oakhaven’s permanent market.

The two undertakings were connected.

As traffic increased along the Greenmere river, tolls, thefts, and competing claims at its crossings became more profitable. Aveline wanted a protected route by which supplies could reach the shore. Local landholders wanted protection without a permanent royal presence capable of examining their own exactions.

The tower settlement of the one hundred and ninety-second year was the compromise already described. Its boundaries were inspected by men who had reason to attend closely. The queen could put a watch above the road. She could not thereby acquire every meadow visible from its roof.

Her officers also confirmed the market at Oakhaven, where carriers and local holders agreed to maintain a landing in return for specified privileges. The family later known as de La Tour had arrived from the Istrian coast during her father’s reign. They possessed hereditary standing before their arrival, but little land. Their first substantial holding in the islands came through marriage and service, confirmed in the two hundred and fifth year.

Wealth helped them defend that position. It did not create their ancestry.

Aveline’s reign was remembered warmly in Greenmere, less warmly by households obliged to provide carts. One carter’s complaint survives because he took his team away while a royal load was still waiting.

He had agreed to carry stone from the quarry to the tower. An officer required him to continue to the coast. His answer, entered by the clerk, was that a road did not become shorter because a queen wished to use it.

The judgment upheld the officer’s right to complete the immediate transport and awarded the carter additional payment. The treasury record suggests that he waited two years for it.

Aveline died in the two hundred and sixteenth year. Her son Osric II inherited both the works completed during her reign and the payments still due upon them.

He maintained the principal crossings and spent heavily upon the houses from which his family sought spiritual favor. His grandson’s steward later complained that much of the best land near Harth belonged to the dead.

The dead did not cultivate it. Tenants did, under arrangements differing from those on neighboring estates.

This growth of religious property made the kingdom more intricately connected. A house on Sere might hold a meadow on Maroven, receive rents in a chartered town, and possess a claim upon a mill whose lord owed military service to the Crown. Damage to that mill could become a dispute involving four authorities before anyone began repairing the wheel.

The Crown sometimes gained by these connections. It sometimes discovered that a campaign against an opponent was also a campaign against its own creditors.

Under Maeric IV and Edric II, the chancery attempted to make revenues more predictable. Local measures were compared, records copied, and disputed services investigated. Stewards who profited from uncertainty did not always welcome the investigation. Neither did tenants whose favorable arrangements depended upon a memory more generous than the surviving document.

The kingdom grew richer in some districts. The records grew larger almost everywhere.

Chapter Seven — The Years of Wet Bread

Sarra I succeeded her father Edric II in the three hundred and eleventh year. Her son Osric III followed in the three hundred and thirty-ninth.

The great hunger began twelve years into his reign.

Rain had damaged the previous harvest. The next year’s seed was poor, animals weakened, and roads became difficult just when provisions were most needed. An agreement to deliver grain could not make damaged grain sound. Men who had lent seed expected repayment from households which had eaten part of what they were meant to sow.

The hunger varied sharply. Some estates retained reserves. A port with a favorable wind received a cargo while a settlement a short distance inland went without. The wealthy could pay more, but only if there was food within reach and someone willing to bring it.

At Oakhaven, a religious household sold part of its plate to purchase grain. Its record names the donor who replaced the vessels afterward and omits the men who refused to sell food at the earlier price.

On Ordel, a reeve listed three empty holdings beside the same field. The first family had left. The second had died. Of the third, he wrote only that their whereabouts were unknown and that their arrears remained.

Osric III prohibited the removal of grain from several royal districts. Local lords issued competing commands. Carriers detained under one authority sought payment from another. A vessel seized to supply a garrison was not available to carry food for the town whose men had been summoned to defend it.

There were attacks on granaries and executions for theft.

A woman called Hedd was pardoned after taking grain from a store maintained by a royal officer. The pardon described her as moved by necessity. The same officer’s account claimed compensation for what she had taken. Both documents could be valid within their own purposes.

The worst years lasted from the three hundred and fifty-first to the three hundred and fifty-fourth. Recovery took longer.

Surviving households combined holdings they could cultivate. Lords reduced some demands to attract tenants and attempted to preserve others lest concessions become permanent. Children entered service farther from home. Marriages joined property that would once have supported separate households.

The Crown’s revenues diminished while its obligations continued.

When Maeric V inherited in the three hundred and sixty-seventh year, he received a realm in which many grievances had acquired the weight of custom. Some lords wanted to restore services suspended during the hunger. Some tenants believed that services which had ceased should remain extinct. Religious houses possessed claims purchased from desperate families and promises made by rulers who could no longer meet them.

The new king borrowed to restore defenses and sought additional contributions from estates which had recovered unevenly.

He died in the three hundred and eighty-eighth year without an acknowledged son.

His daughter Elene was twenty-three. Her claim was strong. The circumstances in which it would be heard were disastrous.

Book IV — Faith, Land, and Law

Chapter Eight — The Keeping of the Witness

The oldest version of the Witness’s death contains no account of a wonder.

A teacher was required to swear that a man had been present when he had not. The teacher refused, was imprisoned, and died before the hearing. His followers kept his words and gathered to remember him. In the southern recension the teacher is a woman. In an early western account there are two witnesses, one of whom survives.

The name by which the tradition is now known does not settle these differences. The Common Observance teaches that there is one Maker, that the powerful as well as the weak must answer for their conduct, and that a false oath injures the community which relies upon it. Its teachers dispute the nature of that reckoning, the authority of particular writings, and the means by which a person may be reconciled after wrongdoing.

I was taught the Caerwyn reading. I have heard the western reading recited with equal conviction beside the bed of a dying man. It is not my purpose to determine here whose prayer was better received.

Long before the Crown, houses of the Observance offered lodging, maintained burial grounds, and preserved agreements. Their usefulness brought gifts. Gifts brought land. Land brought disputes which could not be resolved solely by exhortations to truthfulness.

At Harth, the keepers became the custodians of royal undertakings. Their successors claimed that the Crown settlement demonstrated the king’s submission to the Witness. Royal writers answered that the king had chosen Harth because its position was convenient and its keeper trustworthy.

A house could believe sincerely in its religious authority and benefit materially from the belief.

On Sere, remembrance of the dead retained strong household forms. Families tended particular graves, brought food to annual gatherings, and argued over who should speak the names. The houses at Merrow accommodated many of these customs. Clergy sent from Caerwyn sometimes called them corruptions.

The western keepers replied that a man unable to pronounce the names of the dead should be cautious about correcting their descendants.

On Ordel, religious institutions accumulated grain land and maintained schools whose clerks served the greater households. Their teachers emphasized the keeping of promises, including promises of service. A tenant summoned to repair a house’s wall might reasonably hear this teaching differently from the keeper receiving the labor.

In Rusk, small houses attached to mining settlements depended upon men whose work injured and killed them. There the yearly remembrance includes a recitation of those lost underground. A refusal by a landholder to release a family from an impossible debt could become the subject of a sermon long before it became a case in court.

These differences do not arrange the faithful into convenient political companies. A Crown supporter may distrust the keeper at Harth. A miner opposing a royal officer may send his son to a royal school. The same religious house can shelter fugitives, collect rents harshly, and preserve the only surviving proof of a poor widow’s holding.

The greater religious councils have repeatedly attempted to regularize practice.

The Council of Harth in the one hundred and sixty-eighth year approved a common form for recording oaths and disputed the authority of hereditary local keepers. Some houses complied. Some accepted the form and retained their keepers. On Vey, a community sent a reply stating that it would consider the matter when a cleric could reach them without requiring a pilot.

The pilot’s payment was left unstated.

In later centuries the office of First Keeper at Harth acquired influence over appointments, marriage judgments, and disputes concerning religious property. It never acquired uncontested command over every island institution. Noble patrons defended their customary rights in foundations. Towns resisted demands upon their charitable funds. Local clergy disliked appointments made by persons who had never seen the parish.

Aldous’s lawful coronation depended in part upon religious acknowledgment. This did not put every religious judgment within his gift. Nor did a keeper’s rebuke release every subject from an obligation to the Crown.

Both sides have frequently claimed otherwise when the occasion was useful.

The popular tales are less restrained.

They tell of candles burning through storms, the sick rising after a blessing, and a murdered keeper appearing on a road to accuse his killer. I have found a medical payment in one celebrated cure and two incompatible dates for another. People in the affected households still give thanks. I cannot discover from their gratitude what caused the recovery.

The surviving account of the Witness asks a harder and more ordinary question: what will a person risk rather than swear to something untrue?

Our institutions have not exhausted that question.

Chapter Nine — The Field and the Hearing

In the four hundred and sixty-eighth year, a man named Thel crossed from Ordel to Sere to marry.

He possessed a written discharge from military service, two changes of clothing, and a claim to wages which had not been met. He believed the discharge sufficient to establish that he was free to go. His former lord’s steward believed it said nothing about his obligations as a tenant.

Thel had left a holding cultivated by his mother and brother. The steward demanded a payment for his departure and another connected with the marriage. The bride’s kin answered that no such payments were customary on their part of Sere.

The dispute did not end when the boat touched shore.

A messenger arrived with a copy of an estate entry. The local hearing accepted that the steward had a claim worth examining. It did not accept his authority to take Thel away. The religious house asked to hear the marriage question separately. Thel’s mother was required to appear before the estate court on Ordel.

Three authorities considered different parts of one household’s life.

The marriage eventually went ahead. The departure payment was reduced. The mother’s household agreed to an additional service at harvest, and Thel surrendered part of the wages he still hoped to recover.

No participant regarded this as proof that the law was clear.

The example is useful because writers too readily describe the inhabitants of an island as either free or unfree, as though everyone lived under one arrangement.

On Ordel, some families hold by customary labor. Others render a fixed payment or specified produce. Some obligations attach to a particular holding; others are asserted against persons and their descendants. An estate may claim rights which neighboring estates have abandoned. A town’s charter may protect its inhabitants from certain demands without protecting a newcomer whose status is disputed.

On Sere, households commonly claim wider rights of departure and local consent. This does not mean that poor families there have no debts or powerful neighbors. On Rusk, a man who can leave his home freely may still be bound by an advance received for work. The terms can be harsh without being identical to an Ordel tenant’s obligations.

The Crown has recognized many of these distinctions because earlier rulers promised to preserve them.

A hearing begins with questions which a stranger may not know he must answer. Who is bringing the complaint? Where is the land? What kind of wrong is alleged? Whose custom governs? Which witnesses possess standing to speak?

A royal officer may hear a matter concerning a breach of the king’s peace and leave the underlying tenancy to a lord’s court. A town may exercise a privilege within its bounds and lose the argument a short distance outside them. A religious judgment concerning a marriage may alter an inheritance over which a secular court also claims authority.

A person with money can travel, wait, obtain copies, and retain help. A person without it may have an excellent claim and no practical means to pursue it.

The poor nevertheless use the courts when they can.

Their entries concern hedges cut down, grain withheld, borrowed animals, disputed boundaries, damaged clothes, and insults which threatened a person’s standing among neighbors. One record describes a woman bringing a complaint about a pan. Its small value did not make it dispensable in her household.

A court can preserve customary limits upon a lord. It can also make a lord’s demands more effective by recording them.

Women’s position varies with status, locality, and the property concerned. Widowhood often allows a woman to act in matters where marriage had required her husband’s participation. In some places, daughters divide an inheritance when there is no son; elsewhere an elder daughter takes a particular holding, or local custom preserves it for one successor. A marriage settlement may protect revenues which neither spouse could simply sell.

Such arrangements help explain wars which are later attributed to pride alone.

Pride was present. So were mills, dowers, wardships, arrears, and the people living upon the disputed land.

Chapter Ten — Men in Another Man’s Colors

A hired man-at-arms can eat at a noble table and remain a commoner.

He may own a horse, possess good mail, command less experienced men, and be trusted with a gate. His service can win money and a reputation. Knighthood, if conferred upon him, changes his honors and expectations. It does not insert noble grandparents into his lineage.

The kingdom’s hereditary families defend this distinction closely.

The rare letters which have altered a commoner’s inherited standing are remembered precisely because they are rare. The grant made to the ancestors of House Kell in the two hundred and seventy-fourth year required royal assent and acknowledgment by holders whose own rights were affected. It rewarded prolonged service during a crisis, but was also secured by a marriage negotiation and the settlement of contested coastal claims.

Many men have rendered service as dangerous without receiving such a grant.

Military employment takes several forms.

Some holders owe service because of the land they possess. Great households maintain experienced followers who serve for wages, maintenance, patronage, or a combination of these. Towns call upon inhabitants under their own privileges and obligations. Captains assemble companies for an agreed campaign and must find another employment when it ends.

One man may pass through several such arrangements during his life.

A levy cannot always be retained until a ruler has finished wanting it. Terms expire. Men seek permission to return to harvest. Horses require fodder, and a large company can consume the available grass near a landing before its transports arrive. A commander who has the right to summon soldiers may lack the money to keep them together.

The records of campaigns contain carpenters, boatmen, smiths, clerks, carters, and men paid to mend what soldiers break. Their presence is easily lost when an account is retold as the deeds of armed lords.

Mercenaries are received according to experience rather than one general rule.

Oakhaven may welcome a company needed to protect shipping and curse the same men when their lodging continues beyond the agreed term. A Rusk settlement remembers soldiers who escorted its grain; another remembers those who took it. The western villages distinguish captains who pay promptly from captains whose promises arrive before armed foragers.

A soldier’s employer matters. So does his conduct.

A man returning to his birthplace in a rival household’s colors may find that his mother’s neighbors make distinctions his captain considers inconvenient. He may also find that those distinctions disappear when a barn is burning.

The treatment of hired men becomes most dangerous at the conclusion of a war. A captain dismissed without full payment still has followers to feed. Their weapons do not vanish with the contract.

This difficulty outlived the great hunger.

It was waiting when Maeric V died.

Book V — The Hundred Years of Unquiet

Chapter Eleven — The Daughter and the Cousin

Elene was the nearest surviving child of Maeric V. Aldous, her father’s cousin, was an experienced adult with a household capable of acting immediately.

The king’s death in the three hundred and eighty-eighth year gave each an advantage which the other lacked.

Elene held a direct hereditary claim. Aldous held troops already assembled under his command, access to the principal royal landing, and the cooperation of officers who feared that a disputed succession would leave their wages unpaid.

Several great holders supported Elene. Others claimed that the kingdom required a man capable of leading its defense. A third group was less concerned with the ruler’s sex than with the confirmations a new ruler could be persuaded to issue.

No party confined its reasoning to the argument afterward recorded in its favor.

The first armed seizure concerned a store of grain.

A captain acting for Aldous detained provisions intended for Elene’s supporters. He described them as royal property awaiting lawful direction. The carriers had contracts naming a different recipient. The local lord took their side, partly because failure to deliver would leave his own men unfed.

Two boatmen died. One had come from an island whose lord had not yet declared for either claimant.

Retaliation followed before there was any agreement about who could lawfully order it.

Elene’s marriage to the Terveth lord Dain Varle brought her ships and a defensible place of residence. It also made other supporters uneasy. A queen married into that family might strengthen Terveth’s claims to influence over the passages. Some holders who had defended female succession began to question the marriage on grounds of public danger.

Aldous promised that local rights would be confirmed and the costs of his supporters’ service recognized.

These promises made him king.

They also encumbered the revenues with which he would have to rule.

The campaign later called the War of the Two Seals did not consist of one continuous battle between two united armies. It moved among landings, estates, and kinship connections. A castle acknowledged Aldous while a neighboring town supplied Elene. A religious house sheltered persons from both sides and was accused of favoring whichever one was absent.

In the three hundred and ninety-first year, Aldous’s men besieged Elene’s supporters at Dunmere on Terveth.

Their ships brought grain but insufficient fodder. Bad weather delayed replacements. Men sickened in the camp. Dain Varle avoided a pitched encounter, attacked small parties gathering provisions, and kept the harbor approaches under observation.

Aldous withdrew before winter.

The withdrawal was expensive enough to change the war. He required contributions from men who had expected a short settlement of the succession. Elene’s allies needed payment for keeping ships away from their ordinary trade. Captains crossed between employers. Their former patrons called the act treachery; their contracts occasionally supported them.

A knight named Herward Merl changed sides after the Crown refused to ransom his brother. The refusal was defended on the ground that the brother had been captured outside the king’s service. Herward had understood the obligation differently.

He burned the landing from which his brother’s captors were supplied.

The fire destroyed goods belonging to men who had tried to arrange the ransom.

Chapter Twelve — A Marriage in Winter

By the four hundredth year, neither claimant could cheaply obtain a complete victory.

Aldous possessed the acknowledged kingship in much of the realm and the means of continuing his household. Elene retained a hereditary claim which his victories had not extinguished. Her son Edric, born in the three hundred and eighty-ninth year, was growing old enough to become the center of another round of promises.

Aldous’s daughter Ysabet had been born in the three hundred and seventy-first year. She possessed estates through her marriage settlement and had been widowed. Her participation in the succession question was not merely that of a daughter waiting for instruction.

She had seen what the war cost her holdings.

The negotiations which eventually joined the two claims took years. Religious authorities considered the kinship of the intended spouses. Holders disputed the provision of estates, the order of succession, and guarantees to persons who had served the unsuccessful side. Elene refused a settlement which preserved her son’s ancestry while depriving him of any enforceable expectation.

Aldous refused to set aside his daughter.

The agreement reached in the four hundred and tenth year provided for the marriage of Ysabet and Edric. Ysabet would succeed her father. Edric’s claim would be acknowledged in the succession after her. Their lawful children would unite the two lines.

Elene renounced the immediate exercise of her claim under conditions which required confirmation of estates and protection for named adherents.

She did not confess that her father had been wrong to name her.

The marriage took place in winter because further delay threatened the settlement. Ysabet was thirty-nine and Edric twenty-one. Later writers found the difference useful for stories about each of them. Their correspondence concerns household officers, estates, and the removal of men who had grown accustomed to profiting from the quarrel.

They had a son in the four hundred and twelfth year. He was named Aldous.

The old king died that same year. Ysabet succeeded in accordance with the agreement.

The birth was celebrated as an answer to prayer. I can verify the payment to the women attending the queen and the provision of cloth for the infant’s household. The prayer belongs to those who offered it.

Ysabet ruled until the four hundred and twenty-first year. Her husband then succeeded as Edric III.

The joined succession ended the principal hereditary contest. Men sometimes speak as though it should therefore have ended the war.

It could not restore every burned mill, determine every confiscated holding, settle every unpaid account, or give every displaced family the land of a rival who had occupied it for twenty years.

Those who had gained most from the conflict had particular reasons to oppose an indiscriminate return to earlier conditions.

Chapter Thirteen — The Wars That Kept Their Names

Edric III spent much of his reign hearing claims produced by the peace.

A lord who had supported Elene sought the restoration of an estate. Its present holder had received it from Aldous I in return for service. The estate’s tenants had married, divided holdings, rebuilt houses, and assumed obligations under the new holder. A judgment could decide which lord prevailed. It could not return the district to the morning before the seizure.

The Greyhaven Articles of the four hundred and twenty-sixth year attempted to distinguish restorations, compensation, and claims too old or uncertain to reopen. They appointed hearings and required securities for compliance.

They also allowed exceptions.

A powerful holder who secured an exception could retain what a weaker one had to surrender. The resulting settlements made the greater peace possible while leaving particular neighborhoods angry enough to fight.

The Small Boundary War on eastern Maroven began in the four hundred and thirty-first year over the passage to a mill. It drew in families whose claims originated during the succession fighting. After two seasons of damage, the parties agreed to an arrangement closely resembling one proposed before the first houses were burned.

Its principal difference was the list of people no longer alive to benefit.

Edric III died of fever in the four hundred and thirty-eighth year. His son Aldous II, aged twenty-six, inherited a legitimate and substantially accepted title. His reign’s difficulties did not arise from a credible rival king.

They arose from the exercise of accepted authority.

He attempted to regularize contributions for coastal defense, inspect contested revenues, and recover payments pledged during the succession crisis. Great holders agreed that the coasts required defense. They disagreed about who should pay, who should command, and whether inspection of an old account authorized collection of a new charge.

In the four hundred and forty-second year, a royal officer detained an Ordel grain shipment while examining arrears claimed from its owner.

The cargo had already been sold.

Its intended recipients included men supplying the garrison on Vey. They withheld their own deliveries until the grain or compensation arrived. Soldiers took food locally. The taking produced resistance. A captain called for assistance, and an inquiry into arrears became a campaign.

The fighting spread unevenly through what came to be called the War of the Winter Granaries.

In some districts it was barely visible. At Harth, the price of lodging increased and petitioners waited longer. On northern Vey, a woman’s testimony describes hiding seed beneath a floor after soldiers had taken the household’s stored food.

The men involved did not all serve the same side. Some carried royal authority. Some belonged to local households. Some had been hired by persons attempting to recover their own detained property.

The crown issued orders against unauthorized seizure while relying upon captains whose wages were late.

Aldous II’s letters show anger at their conduct. The payment rolls show why his anger was insufficient.

The Ordel Settlement of the four hundred and forty-sixth year recognized limits upon certain seizures, provided for disputed accounts to be heard, and assigned particular defense charges to named obligations.

It was neither a general liberation of trade nor an acceptance that the Crown could never act in an emergency.

The meaning of emergency remained available for dispute.

In Rusk, meanwhile, the rising cost of imported provisions strained relations between mine holders, workers, and the households which controlled access to the harbors. Iron could be abundant while bread was scarce. An ore account could show wealth which would not feed a family before the next vessel arrived.

The quarry disturbances of the four hundred and fifty-second year began after the collapse of a timbered gallery.

The workers wanted repairs, compensation, and time in which to bury the dead. The owner’s officers wanted production resumed to meet obligations already undertaken. A religious keeper refused to open the burial ground until a disputed payment was waived. Another keeper opened his own ground instead.

What began as a local grievance acquired supporters for reasons the dead had not shared.

Neighboring holders saw an opportunity to challenge the owner’s rights. Crown officers saw an opportunity to intervene. Carriers raised their charges because the landing was unsafe. Soldiers arrived and required food.

The agreements made after the disturbance secured several improvements and preserved most of the underlying claims. Among the petitioning households was that of a stoneworker whose son was named Morvath.

The future castellan was then thirteen.

Chapter Fourteen — The Peace with Missing Men

The Truce of Ryehaven was sworn in the four hundred and sixty-first year after fighting which had exhausted the households responsible for keeping the western approaches.

Its immediate purpose was modest: to stop seizures, reopen specified passages, release named prisoners, and establish a season in which claims could be heard without fresh damage.

The negotiations took place in a storehouse because no hall near the landing was large enough.

One witness complained that the doors had to remain open to disperse the smell of spoiled fish. Another remembered the cold. Their accounts agree that several captains were kept outside until their employers had discussed the arrears.

The truce was proclaimed before agreement had been reached about paying every company.

It was assumed that the great holders would resume customary defense once the field forces withdrew. Some did. Others disputed the extent of the custom or claimed exemption until their own expenses were recognized.

The captain at the western tower above Stonecross waited for an answer that never arrived.

This was the peace inherited by the young Aldous III when his father died in the four hundred and seventieth year.

He was thirty-seven. His son Edren was nine, his daughter Mara twelve. He had served in campaigns and traveled among holdings whose people knew that the old king’s officers were often unable to enforce what their letters required.

The succession itself was not seriously contested. Those who hoped to alter policy had better means than disputing his birth.

They could delay a contribution, decline a disputed summons, detain a cargo under a competing claim, or promise ships which did not arrive.

The new king sought to restore reliable defense and household discipline. He retained experienced officers and replaced several who had become too closely connected to local interests. Some replacements were necessary. Some removed the very intermediaries capable of persuading those interests to cooperate.

The most bitter campaign of his early reign ended at the Barrow Cut in the four hundred and seventy-sixth year.

An armed company associated with the Order of the Ashen Lance had been sent to keep the eastern road through Greenmere. The order was a fellowship of knights and retainers with its own vows, patrons, and disputes. It possessed no power beyond that of the people who served under its name.

Its men expected provisions from the river landing and support from a royal household company.

The provisions were detained to answer a debt claimed against the supplier. The supporting company was ordered elsewhere after an attack upon a landing needed for the Vey transports. Each decision was defended in the surviving accounts as necessary for another purpose.

Together they did so.

The enemy at the Cut consisted of armed men gathering under rival local protection, together with former soldiers whose allegiance depended upon provisions. They struck the isolated company after rain had made movement difficult.

The retreat became a rout.

Galahalt survived. Many companions did not. The standard was lost, and several of the dead were buried beside an old track through the mounds. The place had been feared long before the fighting; afterward, people had additional names to attach to its stories.

Galahalt says that the royal vanguard withdrew without warning. The surviving orders support his complaint about warning. They do not establish that the officers intended his company’s destruction.

This distinction has brought him little comfort.

The men who later called him the last of the Ashen Lance meant that he alone continued publicly to keep its old obligations. Others survived in different service or abandoned the fellowship. It is easier to sing of the last knight than to account for the men who sold their horses and went home.

The last eleven years have contained periods of comparative quiet. Boats returned to some passages. Damaged roofs were mended. Children grew old enough to work fields their parents had resumed cultivating after the truce.

Elsewhere, the same years brought raids, retaliations, disputed garrisons, and the movement of hungry soldiers between employments.

There has been no single moment when all Aethelgard could reasonably have been told that the war was over.

Book VI — The Reign of Aldous III

Chapter Fifteen — The King's Table

The King eats more slowly than he once did.

Those who have not seen him for several years notice this before they notice the grey in his beard. An old injury troubles one knee. He prefers a smaller chamber when the weather is cold and has acquired the habit of listening to a petition until the speaker begins to repeat himself.

He was born in the four hundred and thirty-third year, when his grandfather Edric III still reigned. His father became Aldous II five years later. His own acknowledgment in the four hundred and seventieth year followed the succession accepted by the principal holders and the authorities whose confirmation custom required.

The Crown’s descent through Ysabet and Elene’s son had already reconciled the competing hereditary lines. His household uses the Talven name; the combined descent through the Varle marriage remains in the pedigrees and settlements. There is no serious uncertainty concerning his identity or lawful place in that descent.

Men who oppose him generally demand that he govern differently.

This makes their resistance more difficult to understand from a distance. A lord may swear loyalty to Aldous and refuse an assessment issued in his name. A town may raise men to keep the king’s peace while excluding a royal officer whom it accuses of violating its charter.

Aldous knows these distinctions. His irritation is often directed at the uses to which they are put.

At a hearing concerning ships, I heard him ask whether a privilege should survive when the men protected by it refused to furnish the service on which it had been granted.

The answer came from a keeper who had supported him in other matters.

“Your Grace must first establish that they have refused what they owe.”

The King dismissed the hearing until the documents could be produced.

He did not imprison the keeper.

His marriage to Eliane of Ordel had been arranged fifteen years before his accession. She belonged to House Morn and brought connections which helped the royal household obtain provisions. Their daughter Mara was born in the four hundred and fifty-eighth year, their son Edren three years later.

Eliane maintained a household of her own. Petitions which struggled to reach the King sometimes came through her officers. This was kindness to some and an unwelcome rival channel to others. Her records include assistance to injured servants, disputed payments to suppliers, and interventions on behalf of people whose greatest advantage was knowing someone she trusted.

She died of fever in the four hundred and eightieth year.

Aldous continued several of her payments after her death. He also allowed some to lapse. Her steward’s attempts to preserve them have created the impression, in certain quarters, that a promise made by a queen should endure longer than the queen’s household.

Princess Mara married Eustace Varle of Terveth in the four hundred and seventy-eighth year. The marriage strengthened a connection without making every Terveth captain a royal dependent. Her husband must consider his own kin, the people furnishing his vessels, and the privileges under which his household holds them.

The King’s daughter is capable of writing to her father on these matters without adopting his officers’ conclusions.

Prince Edren, now twenty-six, has been acknowledged as the intended successor. His place is lawful; his political judgment remains less tested than those who flatter him suggest.

He has served with household troops and traveled among several islands. Some captains find him attentive. Others find him impatient with arrangements whose inconvenience they have spent thirty years learning to manage.

He wants ships to sail when they are summoned.

His father once wanted the same thing with similar certainty.

The King’s court contains advocates of incompatible remedies. Some favor stronger inspection and firmer collection. Some urge the confirmation of privileges in return for immediate assistance. Others would rather wait for a rival household’s difficulties to force compliance.

Waiting has a cost outside the council chamber.

A garrison cannot eat a pending judgment.

Chapter Sixteen — The Grain Already Promised

Chancellor Eamon has been accused of serving only himself by men who have repeatedly asked him for favors.

He came into royal employment through clerical service, not hereditary lordship. His rise has given him access which many better-born men consider an offense in itself. It has also given him opportunities which he has not always refused.

The office he holds is powerful because so many people need a document issued, a payment recognized, an account deferred, or a complaint placed before the right person. He cannot make every request lawful. He can sometimes determine when its lawfulness will be considered.

His defenders say that the kingdom would have ceased paying its soldiers without him.

His enemies ask which soldiers, and through whose intermediaries.

Both questions deserve answers.

The treasury is poor in ready money without being poor in every claim it possesses. Much revenue is promised before it arrives. A payment due at harvest may already support a loan, a household allowance, and an undertaking to repair a landing. Arrears expected from a district damaged by fighting may remain valuable in an account long after collection has become improbable.

When an officer announces that the Crown has funds sufficient for a campaign, the announcement may include money still in the hands of persons resisting the campaign.

Eamon has sought advances against future receipts. The lenders include carriers, noble households, and religious institutions. They expect repayment. Some seek influence over the revenues assigned to them.

This is the condition beneath the dispute at Stonecross.

A composition confirmed in the two hundred and seventy-fourth year is remembered locally as the king’s tithe. It is not identical with the separate religious dues paid by the same households. The composition was reckoned at two hundred account marks annually, falling due in four portions. Later confirmations preserved the amount while leaving room for disagreement over the holdings and services included.

The present demand adds fifty marks for emergency cartage and the keeping of the roads and landings.

Royal officers say that immediate danger justifies the charge. Local witnesses say that customary transport has already been furnished and that an additional assessment requires consent which has not been given.

There are also allegations that the additional receipts are being concealed or diverted.

I have seen accounts in which the same cargo appears under descriptions that do not readily agree. This establishes a reason for inquiry. It does not establish every accusation made in the tavern.

Eamon’s household has benefited from relationships with suppliers. Whether a particular benefit was a fee, a gift, a customary allowance, or an illicit payment can depend upon facts the surviving entry was not intended to disclose.

The dangers he invokes are near at hand. Armed men are moving, stores are inadequate, and the Crown’s creditors are becoming more exacting.

Vivienne de La Tour requires the same grain and the same ships for different undertakings.

She inherited her family’s principal Oakhaven rights after her father Renaud died in the four hundred and sixty-ninth year. Her claim depended upon hereditary title and the instruments confirming it. Her wealth made enforcement easier. It did not release her from the interests of cousins, tenants, creditors, and the authorities whose judgments could affect her estate.

She married Mathieu Dorn in the four hundred and seventy-first year. He died eight years later after an infected wound. Their daughter Alis, now fourteen, is central to negotiations which are described publicly as arrangements for the safety of shipping.

Vivienne has declined several proposed marriages for the girl.

The refusals have been called arrogance, maternal caution, and evidence of a secret design upon the kingdom. Her surviving letters suggest that she wishes to avoid putting her daughter’s inheritance under a household which already owes too much to the Crown.

She favors the League’s common action against seizures and disputed tolls. Its undertakings join shipowners, representatives of chartered towns, and noble protectors, each concerned that another member may gain too much from their cooperation. Several participants want stronger privileges within the monarchy. Others are willing to contemplate a more complete breach. Their agreements do not make them unanimous.

Lady Cecily carries messages and negotiates assurances that Vivienne would prefer not to issue openly. Captain Malor offers armed protection to certain carriers and has been accused of taking goods whose owners did not accept his terms. His employer’s interest in keeping the passages open does not make all his methods lawful.

In Rusk, Castellan Morvath faces a similar shortage without Vivienne’s store of negotiable wealth.

His authority is an office, not an inherited nobility. His right forearm was lost during fighting at a grain landing in the four hundred and sixty-seventh year. The heavy covering he now wears over the injury has encouraged a title which people repeat as though iron had replaced everything beneath it.

He had served as a soldier and an overseer before Lady Beatrice Arven placed him in charge of Dunhollow’s defense in the four hundred and seventy-sixth year. The Crown subsequently confirmed the appointment. Lady Beatrice’s son Edmund was a child. The young heir is now seventeen.

Morvath must preserve property that does not become his because he holds its keys.

The body around him, known as the Iron Council, includes estate officers, holders of mining interests, men concerned with provisions, and religious representatives whose communities bear the consequences of injury and hunger. Its members disagree about the rights of House Arven, the limits of royal wardship, and who should decide what Edmund will inherit.

Some regard Morvath as necessary. Some expect to dismiss him when their own position is secure.

He needs grain, reliable passage, and enough authority to prevent his soldiers from supplying themselves at the expense of the settlements he claims to protect.

Aldous, Vivienne, and Morvath each insist that the others are obstructing necessities upon which ordinary people depend. Each can produce examples.

Their representatives are presently promising the same carriers protection against one another.

Book VII — The Isles and Their Peoples

Chapter Seventeen — Caerwyn

The approach to High Cairn gives a stranger an exaggerated impression of royal order.

The road rises above cultivated land toward a strong enclosure of stone. Officers know where visitors are expected to wait. The gates are kept, horses are taken to their appointed places, and a man carrying a petition can be directed through several courtyards without coming appreciably nearer to the person he wishes to see.

Beyond the immediate approaches, Caerwyn is less regular.

The royal family’s own holdings lie beside estates whose lords preserve rights older than the present walls. House Rauth controls important stretches of the western marsh road. Smaller holders possess mills, ferries, and obligations which cannot be erased by drawing a circle around the capital.

Caerwyn’s court speech is understood widely among officers and substantial merchants. In the northern villages, older words remain common. A clerk educated at Harth can make himself useful across several islands while finding that a witness from the hills has described a boundary in terms no one present knows how to translate.

The island’s people are often accused of confusing the kingdom with the reach of the royal household.

The accusation is understandable at High Cairn and less just at Halewick, where shipwrights watch court officers requisition vessels they have spent months preparing for other owners.

Caerwyn furnishes noble household troops, men owing service through land, and townsmen responsible for particular defenses. Its military strength is substantial, but each part has a history and a limit. The King does not possess all these men in the manner in which he possesses a cloak.

The island’s markets receive provisions from elsewhere. Its dignity depends upon boats arriving.

At the annual remembrance of the Crown settlement, officers walk from the lower hall to Harth carrying copies of the earliest undertakings. Several great families attend. Some send substitutes whose rank communicates the state of their relations with the court more clearly than their speeches.

People from the other islands watch the procession with differing degrees of respect.

The officers carry the documents back afterward. The disputes do not accompany them in any orderly fashion.

Chapter Eighteen — Maroven

Maroven’s wealth is visible from a road in Greenmere only after a person has learned what he is looking at.

The fields are divided among holdings of different kinds. Woodland supplies fuel and timber. Water turns mills, carries boats where the channel allows, and floods ground which an account may describe as reliably productive. The same river can furnish a convenient route and a quarrel over who has obstructed it.

Stonecross stands where several ordinary journeys intersect. People come for the market, the crossing, the forge, a hearing, or a conversation with someone who has news from the coast. Its inhabitants know more about events beyond their fields than outsiders sometimes suppose, though the reports reach them in different forms.

A royal dispatch, a carrier’s complaint, and a refugee’s account can describe the same action without naming it alike.

The Harrow family holds rights in and around Greenmere. Lady Joan succeeded her grandfather Ansel in the four hundred and seventy-eighth year, subject to settlements which preserved interests for other relations. She is a hereditary landholder; Elder Alden’s influence rests upon local standing and usefulness. Neither can speak without qualification for every household in Stonecross.

Oakhaven lies downriver, where cargo is transferred, stored, accounted for, and disputed. The de La Tours’ authority overlaps with the town’s chartered privileges and the interests of carriers who do not live under their courts.

The League’s common cause has not removed these disagreements.

Maroven’s speech varies between the inland settlements and the port. Oakhaven hears more foreign words, particularly in matters of cargo and payment. People inland sometimes imitate the accent when accusing a trader of being too clever. The trader may reply in their own dialect.

Soldiers find an uneven welcome. A company hired to keep the road can bring custom to the tavern and ruin to the households required to lodge it. A man who pays for his food may be welcomed where his employer’s collector is hated.

Greenmere has known years of apparent quiet. Its people have repaired damage and resumed habits that can look permanent to someone arriving in a good season.

The abandoned western tower remains above the road.

Chapter Nineteen — Sere

Sere receives the weather before much of the kingdom does.

Its western shore is broken and exposed. Settlements favor sheltered bays, workable valleys, and places where a boat can be drawn safely above the water. A journey around a headland may require less distance than a journey across it and considerably more patience.

The island’s older speech, called Seiric in the schools, has absorbed words from Istr and from generations of visitors. It survives strongly in households and local hearings. A person answering a royal officer in the court speech may turn to a neighbor in Seiric to determine what the officer has actually requested.

House Sern possesses hereditary rights along important parts of the coast. Lady Eda, its present head, must nevertheless reckon with households whose obligations are settled by local testimony and religious houses holding instruments of their own. Her title does not permit her to appoint every master, dispose of every pasture, or treat all coastal men as one obedient company.

Sere’s annual gatherings preserve public remembrance of agreements. The practice can protect a weak claimant when enough neighbors remember the same terms. It can also expose a stranger to the combined memory of people with an interest in remembering him unfavorably.

The religious houses at Merrow maintain burial records, receive travelers, and mediate disputes. They quarrel with one another about appointments and property as readily as they quarrel with secular holders.

Hired soldiers are remembered locally according to who brought them and how they behaved. There are villages where a captain’s descendant can still obtain lodging because his grandfather paid what he owed. A few miles away, a different company’s name is used to frighten children into closing the door.

These recollections do not determine support for Vivienne or Aldous. Sere’s holders may cooperate with the Crown against a predatory company while resisting an officer’s demand for boats. A household may distrust strangers and still have a son earning his maintenance abroad.

At Bracken Mouth, the old path to the beach remains in use. The present holders would find it burdensome if everyone who claimed an ancient connection came to inspect it.

I mention this because I did.

Chapter Twenty — Ordel

A visitor reaching Ordel after the western islands notices the depth of its settled estates.

There are long-worked fields, substantial religious houses, roads maintained under obligations that can be traced through several generations, and households accustomed to producing documents when another authority makes a demand.

The island is fertile without being uniformly prosperous. Poor ground, divided holdings, bad seasons, and unequal obligations exist beside the richer manors. Grain sent away from a landing may belong to a lord, a religious house, a merchant who has purchased it in advance, or several people whose interests are not apparent from the sacks.

The Morns are the greatest noble household, but they do not own Ordel entire. Count Bertrand Morn must consider lesser hereditary holders, institutions with exemptions, and the towns whose charters limit his officers.

The late Queen Eliane’s kinship makes him important to the Crown. It does not oblige him to accept every interpretation of a royal charge.

Customary tenants in some districts owe substantial labor and restrictions upon departure. Elsewhere, arrangements allow fixed payments or more favorable conditions. Caldmere’s charter provides particular protections for inhabitants admitted under its terms. It does not establish that every person arriving at the gate has thereby extinguished an obligation elsewhere.

The island’s religious schools send clerks into noble and royal employment. Their written language is close to the form used at court, while household speech retains distinctions that educated men sometimes conceal when traveling.

Ordel supplies armored household men, levies raised under local obligations, and provisions for forces assembled elsewhere. The supplying of grain has given its holders influence beyond what a count of soldiers would suggest.

It has also made them targets for resentment.

People who watched an Ordel vessel refuse a hungry landing seldom knew who owned the cargo or what undertaking bound its master. They remembered that it sailed on.

In the religious houses, the yearly remembrance of the hunger includes a meal furnished for those who cannot pay. Some recipients come from holdings which owe services to the institution feeding them.

They may be grateful without considering the account settled.

Chapter Twenty-One — Rusk

The sounds of Dunhollow reach the road before its walls come into view.

There are hammers, carts, animals under load, and men calling from places where the slope prevents them seeing one another. Smoke rises from work requiring charcoal and carefully maintained heat. The woods from which fuel comes are consequently a source of disputes as serious as those concerning the ore.

The island contains farmland and fishing settlements as well as mines and quarries. Its difficulty is not that no food can be grown there. Population, work, terrain, and the distribution of holdings have made some important districts dependent upon provisions brought from elsewhere.

A prosperous undertaking can therefore stand beside hungry households.

The Arvens’ hereditary position is old. Their authority has been defended through marriages, confirmed grants, local courts, and the military value of the defenses they hold. Morvath’s castellan’s office operates within that inheritance while the young Edmund approaches the point at which he can claim its fuller exercise.

The men around the Iron Council do not all desire the same future.

Some want an heir capable of defending their arrangements against royal interference. Some want more influence over the revenues and appointments of his estate. Some want reliable payment and bread. Others expect Morvath to remain necessary after every formal question is settled.

The island’s speech is harsh to a Caerwyn ear chiefly because the Caerwyn listener does not understand it. I have heard a Rusk boatman sing to a frightened child with great patience. A stereotype based upon the sound of men shouting above hammers would have done him little justice.

Military service draws upon noble households, local levies, hired companies, and men retained to defend particular works and approaches. Spears, bows, crossbows, and mail are more useful descriptions of their means than the elaborate machines imagined by distant storytellers.

A company which brings grain safely into a hungry district may be remembered favorably even if its employer is disliked. One which takes grain on the road can acquire enemies among people who otherwise share its patron’s cause.

At the remembrance of the gallery collapse, names are spoken in the order supplied by the surviving households. There is still a disagreement over two men whose employer described them as absent.

Their families say they were below.

Chapter Twenty-Two — Terveth

Terveth sits beside passages whose usefulness depends upon knowing when not to enter them.

Its carriers possess knowledge accumulated through voyages, observation, and losses. A master may refuse to sail in weather which appears tolerable to an impatient passenger. The passenger sees delay. The master remembers a particular rock that will be hidden when they reach it.

House Varle has drawn much of its influence from its connections with these coastal interests. Its hereditary holdings are substantial, but the boats supporting its power belong under varying arrangements to households, partnerships, and persons whose claims a lord must respect if he wishes them to continue supplying him.

Women appear in those claims more frequently than outsiders expect. A widow may retain shares through a settlement or inheritance. A daughter’s right to a particular interest need not follow the same rule as succession to an estate.

The islanders are not thereby free of hierarchy. A poor seaman has less influence over a voyage than the owners who can replace him. A creditor may exercise a power a noble officer cannot easily imitate.

Lady Sarra Varle, now head of her branch of the house, has spent years preserving obligations that other people would prefer to interpret more generously. Her son Eustace’s marriage to Princess Mara gives the family access to royal counsel and royal requests. It also means that its refusals are noticed more closely.

Terveth’s port speech is shaped by exchange with the Istrian coast and the other islands. People who use several tongues in a day are sometimes accused of having several loyalties. The accusation is useful whenever a cargo goes to the wrong recipient.

The island’s military contributions include ships, crews, armed households, and men serving for specified periods. Putting warriors aboard does not turn a transport into a vessel that can safely do anything a commander imagines.

Some hired captains are admired for protecting the passages. Others are called sea thieves. A commission can affect the lawfulness of a seizure without comforting the person whose goods have been taken.

Terveth’s people understand this distinction well enough to argue both sides of it.

Chapter Twenty-Three — Vey

On Vey, a promise of assistance is judged partly by the season in which it is made.

The outer island lies beyond Rusk’s approaches, exposed to weather that can keep a vessel from landing even after it has reached the shore. Its people live by fishing, small holdings, animals suited to poor grazing, and the work of pilots. They preserve close connections with relatives and employers elsewhere.

House Kell holds the principal hereditary dignity. The ancestor whose standing was formally enlarged in the two hundred and seventy-fourth year had served the Crown through repeated campaigns and helped settle disputed coastal claims. His descendants have used that grant to defend privileges which the descendants of older houses sometimes describe as excessively generous.

The difference in age between two noble houses is not a comfort to a tenant whose obligations are much the same under either.

Vey’s customs limit some services because the island cannot furnish them at all times. The written exceptions have become important to later disputes about ships and watch duties. Royal officers suspect convenient appeals to weather. The islanders suspect officers who count boats more readily than days in which boats can sail.

The northern settlements suffered severely during the War of the Winter Granaries. Several were rebuilt farther from exposed landings. An old chapel now stands beyond the houses that formerly surrounded it. The burial ground remains in use, requiring a walk which can be difficult in winter.

People disagree about who abandoned whom during the fighting.

The keeper whose records I consulted had copied the names of the dead from household testimony and written beside several entries that the cause was uncertain. A later hand supplied the word slain. The keeper’s original account includes hunger and sickness.

Vey hires soldiers when it can afford them. It dislikes being required to support men who cannot be recalled or dismissed by the households furnishing their food. This attitude has produced cooperation with the Crown in one season and determined resistance in another.

At the small landing of North Ness, an elderly pilot showed me the place where a supply vessel had waited offshore until its water was nearly gone. He remembered the ship’s name and the direction of the wind.

He did not remember which minister had issued its orders.

Book VIII — The Kingdom on the Eve

Chapter Twenty-Four — The Messages Ahead of the Men

During the autumn in which I began setting these books in order, three messengers left High Cairn for the eastern landings.

One carried a summons. One carried an explanation of the summons. The third carried private assurances that several persons named in it need not fear the interpretation placed upon it by the first.

The first messenger reached his destination last.

At Oakhaven, merchants were asking which undertakings would be honored if cargo were detained by the Crown and then recovered by men acting for the League. Some refused further voyages without additional guarantees. Others accepted the risk because delay would ruin them as surely as seizure.

The taverns filled with accounts of what particular captains had done. The accounts grew firmer as the witnesses grew more distant.

In Dunhollow, officers inspected stores which looked more substantial in the last report than they did when the doors were opened. A reeve explained that some provisions had been promised and others were already assigned. Morvath asked what could be eaten before the next ship arrived.

The distinction was entered in the margin.

At High Cairn, the King’s counselors considered whether a refusal to furnish ships constituted resistance to lawful authority or a dispute requiring further hearing. The men responsible for the garrisons said that further hearing would not maintain them through winter.

The King asked for the original undertakings.

Someone sent for me.

There are districts where these questions still seem remote. Ploughing continues. Marriages are arranged. People repair roofs, buy animals, complain about neighbors, and put money aside against obligations they expect to meet. Their lives are not suspended while powerful households decide how far a refusal can be pressed.

Neither are the households waiting upon one unknown man’s decision to take service.

A captain who cannot obtain an answer will find another patron. A holder who fears losing the harvest will move provisions behind walls. Carriers will choose safer landings. Refugees will arrive where there is still food, placing new demands upon people already anxious about its price.

Each action can be reasonable in itself.

Together they may leave a garrison isolated, a road closed, a market empty, and a settlement exposed to men who have ceased expecting wages.

I do not know which of the present assurances will hold. I know that several rely upon persons who have given different assurances elsewhere.

The kingdom has survived such circumstances before. It has also lost much that its inhabitants once thought permanent.

Chapter Twenty-Five — A Lodging in Stonecross

The Boar & Flagon had smoke in its rafters and damp cloaks hanging wherever a peg could be found.

At a table near the wall, Scribe Pippin was examining a scrap of writing while Wulf explained something with the patience of a man certain that the account favored him. Sir Balin sat with his bad knee extended beneath the bench. He had been discussing a challenge made on an earlier evening and had reached the part of the discussion in which the original words were becoming less important than who had heard them.

Margery sang a verse about an officer whose horse ate better than his escort. Two of the men who laughed were wearing the officer’s colors.

Hob Ashby asked whether anyone required a dependable soldier. When this produced no immediate offer, he began to describe the sort of employer whom a dependable soldier should avoid.

Outside, a carrier was reluctant to unload until he knew whether he would be required to wait. The stable boys had their own dispute concerning the space available.

The next morning I went into the market.

Beatrice was selling bread and keeping aside several loaves which did not enter the same account. A woman newly arrived from a damaged settlement asked whether work could be found. Beatrice directed her to a household which needed help and warned her to establish the payment before beginning.

Giles wanted to speak about an animal. Alden wanted to speak about the new demand. Each believed his difficulty had been postponed long enough.

At the forge, Odo was examining iron brought by a man who insisted that its quality had been misrepresented. The noise made prolonged deception difficult but did not prevent people attempting it. A young assistant carried water and waited for an opportunity to ask about his own wages.

Brother Dunstan had gone to visit someone sick. At the chapel, Agnes was changing a bandage and telling her patient to stop moving. She had no patience for his claim that a brave man should be able to endure pain while standing.

She required him to sit.

Beyond the village, the old paths continued among holdings whose boundaries could be explained only by the people living beside them. Travelers used a northern stone gateway left from an abandoned enclosure. Some called the place unlucky. An older man said that the trouble had begun when the roof was taken down and people started carrying away the wall.

The burial mounds farther east had acquired stories from several centuries. There were reports of lights and sounds, of men seen where men had no honest business, of animals frightened on the road. The reports did not establish the nature of the danger. A person could lose his purse to a living man while worrying about a dead one.

Galahalt kept his camp near the ruined watchtower. He was mending a strap when I passed.

He asked whether I had found the order concerning the withdrawal at the Cut.

I said that I had found an order. There were still questions about when it had arrived.

He nodded as though the distinction had been made to him before.

That evening a man came into Stonecross by the western road.

He carried his own bundle and wore equipment repaired in several places. His manner around other armed men suggested experience. There was no device upon his clothing to announce a hereditary household of his own, and he gave no title when the woman at the lodging house asked his name.

“Rowan.”

She asked whether someone would answer for his payment.

He said that he could pay for a bed and one meal.

It was sufficient for the night.

A boy showed him where to put his belongings. At the hearth, two carriers were arguing about a crossing he had never used. Someone mentioned a captain looking for men. Someone else said that the captain’s last company had not been paid.

Rowan listened.

In the morning he would have to decide what to do with the money that remained, whom to believe, and whether an offer of work could be trusted. Whatever he chose, the messengers would continue their journeys. Men already under orders would march or refuse. A boat would leave Oakhaven when its master judged the wind suitable, carrying provisions that several people expected to receive.

The woman brought Rowan a bowl.

He moved his bundle beneath the bench to make room for another traveler.

Here ends the account of the kingdom before the present troubles have declared their full extent.

Appendix G — Cultures and Institutions

Caerwyn’s courtly speech, Sere’s Seiric, the idioms of Terveth’s carriers, and the other regional usages are working distinctions, not fully constructed languages. Intermarriage, travel, service, and translation prevent neat borders between populations.

The Common Observance, its Witness traditions, the House at Harth, the House at Merrow, and the office of First Keeper are proposed religious material. Shared oaths coexist with local burial, commemoration, festival, patronage, and appointment customs. Nothing in the book establishes a miracle as objectively real or resolves the owner’s theological decision.

Tenure varies within islands as well as between them. Thel’s case, Caldmere’s charter, Sere’s household rights, and Stonecross’s composition are examples within a provisional economic and legal reconstruction. They do not approve a kingdom-wide economic model. No border crossing automatically extinguishes a personal or landed obligation.

Military service is likewise mixed. Levies, household retainers, hired companies, and town defenses can operate in the same campaign under different terms. A mercenary’s welcome depends upon payment, conduct, remembered losses, and present security; it does not dictate the host community’s civil-war allegiance.

Appendix H — Questions the Surviving Records Cannot Settle

The uncertainty around Nera concerns the voyage’s date, her earlier life, and the identities of people whom later genealogies omitted. It supplies no evidence of secret royal blood for Rowan.

The meaning first attached to the name Aethelgard remains disputed. Later explanations may reveal more about their authors’ politics than about the original speakers.

At Barrow Cut, the sequence of warnings, changed orders, and supply decisions can be reconstructed only imperfectly. A deliberate sacrifice of the Ashen Lance is an allegation, not an established fact.

The Stonecross aid requires a comparison of promised money, received money, actual cartage, and prior obligations. A missing receipt does not by itself prove theft; the inability to obtain one may nevertheless injure the payer.

The western tower’s foundation is documented more clearly than the last practical decisions to leave it empty. Different authorities preserved the claim to payment while responsibility for useful maintenance became difficult to enforce.

The abandoned chapel on Vey, the northern gateway, and reports around burial mounds invite investigation into settlement, weather, theft, memory, and human use. They are not concealed permissions to introduce supernatural phenomena.

Appendix I — The Boundary Between Canon and Proposal

LOCKED CANON. The twelve owner-approved constraints govern the whole manuscript: a nonsupernatural high-medieval archipelago; Aldous III’s legitimate, limited feudal monarchy; regional religion with theology undecided; hereditary aristocracy distinct from knighthood; overlapping jurisdictions; varied obligations that survive travel; distinct island cultures; a century of intermittent violence; Rowan’s common-born, experienced, unknighted origins; mixed military institutions; and locally contingent attitudes toward mercenaries.

OPEN DECISION — Rule 13. The kingdom’s economic organization remains undecided. The book proposes interacting rents, labor services, tolls, dues, wages, credit, market transactions, and requisitions to make events causally intelligible. Neither their combination nor the island assignments are approved by appearing in this manuscript.

PROPOSED HISTORY REQUIRING OWNER APPROVAL. This includes the seven named major islands and their relative positions; their settlements and languages; the Crown calendar and present year; Oren and his sources; all ancient peoples and migration stories; the entire early dynasty and the cousin relationship supplied for Aldous I; the wars, treaties, marriages, and campaign dates; the noble-house register; the detailed biographies and family relationships assigned to existing characters; regional tenures, charters, religious institutions, and military arrangements; the Stonecross figures and emergency demand; and every newly invented ordinary inhabitant.

PROPOSED THEOLOGY REQUIRING OWNER APPROVAL. The Maker, Witness, Common Observance, forms of judgment and oath, institutional offices, and local accommodations are one possible religious foundation. A future approved theology may require substantive revision without changing the nonsupernatural rule.

ESTABLISHED FUTURE DIRECTION, NOT COMPLETED HISTORY. The coming civil war develops through independent decisions, resources, local conflicts, and accumulating consequences. The chronicle stops before its outcome. It preserves room for four planned endings, including a catastrophic collapse if intervention proves insufficient, without choosing an ending in advance or making the kingdom’s history depend upon Rowan’s arrival.

This volume makes no claims about which systems are implemented in the native GameMaker project. Its historical proposals must be reconciled with approved canon before anyone treats them as instructions to change the game.

Editorial Note on Historical Research

The kingdom, people, chronology, quoted dialogue, letters, songs, and archival extracts in this volume are fictional. Historical research informed the treatment of institutions and material constraints; it is not evidence that Aethelgard existed.

Useful primary texts and archive guides include the 1215 text of Magna Carta, Yale Law School’s Avalon Project, particularly its treatment of inheritance, wardship, custom, obligation, and the limits of taking supplies; The National Archives’ guides to manors and manorial records and medieval and early modern soldiers; and the Metropolitan Museum of Art’s account of arms and armor in medieval Europe.

These are points of comparison rather than a single template. The fictional islands deliberately possess different customs. The military material assumes the importance of mail, helmets, shields, padded protection, transport, fodder, provisions, and payment; it does not import the widespread complete plate harness of a later period.
